## 1. THE RUNEFALL (the story)

*Such is the telling at the Whiterun hearths, nearly a year past, and getting
older with every telling.*

### The night the crystal came

Late in Frostfall, in the year 4E 200, the plains south of Whiterun lay
under a hard frost when a light crossed the sky. The shepherds first
took it for a shooting star of unusual size — then saw it did not
streak. It *descended*, slow as a lid settling. Its color was wrong for
fire: green-white, sea-cold, like dawn seen from beneath water.

It struck open ground within sight of the city's walls, far enough that
no stones reached the farms, close enough that the wall-top guards
watched it land plainly. Old Whiterun swears the sound was not a roar
but a *chime* — a single deep note, like a held breath finally let out.

The crater held a black crystal taller than a longhouse, veined with
cold silver light that moved slowly, the way weather moves. It was
hoarfrost-cold in autumn. Birds would not cross over it. No hammer
was ever brought to it; on the ninth night it broke of its own design —
or of someone else's, no one can say. Witnesses say it came apart with
a sound like a hundred bells heard through deep water, and out of it
flew hundreds of bright splinters: things now plainly called **runes**.

A farmhand who watched them pour out of the sky said it plainly and
jeeringly: "*Runes, fell. Runefall.*" The name stuck to the whole event,
and eventually to everything that grew from it.

### The runes

Each rune is a splinter of the fallen glass, small as a knucklebone,
cased in cold light and marked with a single burning syllable — not a
letter of any alphabet written in Tamriel, and not the dragon tongue
that shouts use. Men of this land already know that power can live in a
spoken word; the Voice is proof. These are old words made new, though:
words whose grammar no one has learned, walking around in a splinter of
glass that fell from a hole in the sky.

They did not scatter like shrapnel. They *drifted* — some on the wind,
some against it, as if choosing. A shepherd woke with one curled in the
fold of his blanket. Wolves bit them out of the ground out of
curiosity. The White River carried three of them down toward its falls
in a single day.

### What a rune does

The first finders died of it: the graves of the fall's first week hold
two shepherds, a farmer, and two mill-road bandits who thought the
bright stones were for sale. But a rune does not work through the
hand. It roots in the body that keeps it.

- A rune will **root** in a living thing that survives its first touch.
  Thereafter it shows as a pale scar of light — bare skin, fang,
  antler, horn, the flank of a cave bear glowing faintly through winter
  fur. Only natural flesh will take one: the long-dead, the dragons, the
  Daedra and the Dwemer's iron servants refuse it — nothing in them for
  it to feed on, or a grammar of their own already.
- A rune left **bare** will try the first warm flesh that holds it:
  it either takes root or is taken — the graves of the fall's first
  week are what trying looks like. So hunters keep harvested runes
  sealed in iron-bound cases: iron cannot cut a rune that has rooted,
  but it will keep a loose one sleeping.
- A rooted rune **feeds on the fighting** of its bearer, and when it
  feeds, the bearer grows. Stronger, harder, quicker. The scar
  brightens as the bearer climbs.
- The climb is a **stair**, and hunters who count such
  things give its rungs plain names: Runetouched, Runebound, Runeforged,
  Runecrowned, Runelord, Runebreaker, and the seventh, whispered over
  mugs in the Bannered Mare — *Runefall Cataclysm* — a name for what
  they fear, less than what they have seen. Above the Cataclysm lie the
  three names only the oldest hunters use: Runewarden, Runesovereign,
  and Runefall Ascendant.
- When a rune-bearer dies, **the rune does not die with them**. It lingers in the scar for a few
  breaths, then leaps to the nearest living body strong enough to hold
  it. Hunters race those breaths with a blade; a rune cut free in time is
  *taken*, straight into the iron case. If none is near, it sleeps on the ground as a lump of cold glass, waiting for
  warm flesh to pass. This is why the hunting never ends: every kill
  sends its rune looking for a better host, and most often, the better
  host is something that has already learned to hunt.
- Two runes do not share one body politely. The rune in the blood and
  the rune in the hand will argue, and the body is the rope in the
  tug. The folk wisdom of the hunt passed through Whiterun in the
  matter of a season: *keep the runes in iron, not in blood* — forge them, do not host them.

A rune also has a temperament — hunters read the brands: ember-orange
low sun fire they call **Ember**; pale ice-blue **Rime**; the slow
green of shade-tender growth **Verdancy**; iron-grey **Bastion**;
storm-white **Tempest**; a cold violet dark, **Umbral**; wound-red
**Sanguine**; and the plain cold silver of the fall itself, **Aether**.

### What iron cannot do

Here is the honest arithmetic that turned a wonder into a trade.

A rune that has fed a full year weaves itself into its bearer's hide
and bone. Against that weave, plain iron is *spent* — arrows skate on
antlers of elk that have climbed a dozen rungs, and the wound closes in a breath. The
one thing that bites a rune's weave is another rune, said along its own
seam. The Companions' saying from that first winter: **"A rune answers
only to a stronger rune. You cannot out-muscle a rune's curtain; cut
the curtain's seam."**

So smiths took runes out of dead wolves' and raiders' scars and laid
them into the temper of blades and armor. A rune in iron does what it
does in flesh: the tool eats the fight, the tool grows, and the tool
carries its syllable into the enemy's weave where plain steel cannot.
Hence rune-hunters' gear looks like any gear — a wolf-fang clasp, a
river-stone whetstone, a rune bitten through an old steel cuirass — but
a rune-bound cuirass has closed seams that swallow an ordinary edge,
and only a *better* rune-forged edge parts it again.

### The hunt

By spring of 4E 201 the plains south of Whiterun had become a ladder of
market value. A high-rung wolf's rune, cut free of its carcass, was
worth a flock. Farmers paid hunters to sweep the woodlines; hunters
paid smiths to lay harvested runes into their harness; the wise kept
their own blood rune-free and their iron hungry. Towns did not empty,
for rune-bearers shun the ring of wrought iron and the closeness of a
thousand hearths — the hunts stay outside the walls, where the sky is
open and the settlements' long spears are not.

And hunters learned the rune's own etiquette. Crack the iron case of a harvested rune a thumb's width, speak your quarry aloud into the dusk — the old Nord
oath of the promised hunt, renewed — and a rune-bearer somewhere in the
wind will *answer the resonance* and come to prove itself against the
call. The kill that follows such a call is honored as **proofed**: the
hunt called it, the hunt ended it, the yield is paid whole.

Sometimes no one calls, and still the wind delivers. A rune-beast
arriving uninvited at a traveler's shoulder is called *a hunt given* —
the runes seek sport. Nord custom says honor a hunt given. Pay it,
one way or the other.

### The Stilled

The runes keep a cruel tax on ascent. The rune eats from the same purse
it fills, and past a certain stair it starts taking installment on the
soul. High-stair hunters go before their time: wordless, patient, eyes
filmed with silver markings like the dead glass themselves. Hunters
call them the **Stilled**. Some put
down their iron and walk the wilds to be hunted.

The College sent a loremaster south that first winter and got back a
letter, in a careful hand. It relays a Dwemer rumor: *they found
a similar ore and pressed it into war; it started as a war and ended as
a silence.* The letter's last line, much quoted since: "**I cannot say
what it is. I can say what it does — it makes strength, and takes
possession.**"

### Today

Because a rune keeps its host climbing as long as the host keeps
feeding, the plains do not quiet with time. They steepen. Nearly a year on —
with the second Frostfall still to come — the wolf-lines east of the river have
climbed to rungs their grandsires never saw, the White River
carries rune glass down past the sawmills on its banks, and the
ballads of the Bannered Mare have grown a verse for every stair.

An old woman by the Riverwood sawmill says the fall is not over. "It fell
one mountain of glass," she says, "but watch the dawn. The cold light
never wholly left the sky. Everything since is just the weather the
fall left behind."

Beyond the hold's borders the fall is a farmers' tale. The war between
Imperial and Stormcloak keeps the great houses looking elsewhere, and a
star that broke no wall is easily forgotten from far away.

And this is where the latecomer steps in — you, arriving with the
Whiterun gate behind you and the open plains ahead. The whole hold now
runs on one transaction, spoken in every tone from farmhand to
Companion:

**Every strength buys its next strength from something weaker.
The sky remembers what it dropped. Welcome to the hunt.**

## 2. In-world voices (short excerpts)

**Letter from a College of Winterhold loremaster, Frostfall 4E 200:**

> "I have heard it set beside Aetherium, and I will say only that the
> comparison flatters us both. Whatever Aetherium may be, this is not
> finished: it is *unrefined* — the raw grammar, still capable of
> speech. I would trade my chair for one pebble of it, and thank the
> Eight that no one has offered."

**Carved over a rune-hunter's grave near the western farms:**

> "Here lies a hunter, fourth of the stair.
> The rune went home."

**Firelit verse at the Bannered Mare:**

> "The star fell blind, the star fell cold,
> it broke into a hundred names —
> the wolf wears words, the fox is bold,
> and iron is spent on the plains."

## 3. Lore ↔ system mapping (for quest text and docs)

| System concept | Lore device used here |
| --- | --- |
| 100-stage ladder (10 tiers × 10) | Rune "climbs" as it feeds; hunters count rungs; tier names are folknames coined in the story itself |
| Deterministic stage, not equal power | "Same rung, different animal" is what farmers joke — the rune is a syllable, the body is its grammar |
| Stage-gap threat protection (no one-shots upward) | "Iron is spent" against a rune-woven hide; wounds close; only runes bite runes |
| Penetration from rune-forged equipment | A harvested rune laid into temper carries its syllable into the target's weave; "cut the curtain's seam" |
| Rune cores from kills | The rune "goes home" — lingers in the scar a few breaths; hunters cut it out in time (*taken*) or it leaps to a new host |

| Rare wilderness manifestations | "A hunt given" — the runes seek uninvited sport; omen etiquette |
| Natural flesh only | Runes root only in natural living flesh — beasts and people; the long-dead, dragons, Daedra and Dwemer machines refuse them |
| Encounter safety (no cities/quest areas) | Rune-bearers shun communal hearths and the ring of wrought iron and bell — the hunts stay outside walls |

## 4. Usage and caution notes

- Canon timeline: the fall is dated **Frostfall 4E 200**, about ten months before the vanilla game's start (Last Seed 4E 201), so the hunting
  culture is mature when the player arrives.
- No canon NPC is named or impersonated. The messengers, smiths,
  shepherds and loremasters remain unnamed on purpose — they can be
  replaced later by in-game NPCs, quests or books without rewriting
  the story.
- No claim of content: "Runefall Cataclysm" as tier VII name is
  flavor dread only; nothing here promises a future sky-tearing event
  or new quests in v1.
- The story deliberately leaves the origin unresolved (three in-world
  theories: a tear of Magnus, an aetherial shard some liken to Aetherium, a sky-storm not yet arrived). Keep it unresolved; a
  certain answer would freeze future design.
- When writing in-game load screens or tooltips, do not use the
  mechanical terms ("stage", "penetration", "protection curve");
  use "stair/rung", "the seam", "iron spent". See section 3 for the
  mapping.
- The mod name **Runefall** is the in-world name of the event, coined
  by a farmhand in section 1. This makes the title diegetic.
- Vanilla awareness: no vanilla NPC mentions the fall. Keep it a local,
  rural story (see section 1, *Today*); nothing here asks vanilla
  dialogue to change.
- Draft decisions, open to change: runes root only in natural living
  flesh (no draugr, dragons, Daedra or Dwemer machines); a rune is not a
  soul, so soul-craft and dragon souls do not touch it; the Voice moves
  air, a rune moves flesh. Revisit once GAME-DESIGN.md fixes scaling rules.

## 5. Changes

### v0.1 → v0.2 (audit fixes)

- Timeline: "second Frostfall still to come"; fall is about ten months before vanilla start.
- Aetherium: letter no longer defines it; origin theory softened to "some liken to".
- Fauna/places: moose -> elk; Rorikstead mill -> Riverwood sawmill.
- Rune lifecycle: rune lingers a few breaths, hunters *take* it before it leaps; bare runes root, so harvested runes are kept in iron-bound cases (fixes the palm contradiction; gives "iron, not blood" meaning).
- Terminology: in-world "stage" replaced by stair/rung.
- Hollowed/Hollowing renamed Stilled/Stilling (ties to the Dwemer "silence").
- Added: natural-flesh-only rule, vanilla-awareness note, draft decisions on souls/Voice.

### v0.2 → v0.3 (self-audit residuals)

- Verse set to even tetrameter ("The star fell blind, the star fell cold"; "the wolf wears words, the fox is bold").
- Rooting wording harmonized with the first finders' graves: a bare rune "takes root or is taken".
- A taken rune goes "straight into the iron case" (closes the cutting-hand gap left by the leap rule).
- "Every mill wheel in the hold" → "the sawmills on its banks" (White River mills per UESP: Riverwood, Mixwater; Rorikstead has none).
- Header purpose line now says "stair of rungs", matching the story's terminology rule.
