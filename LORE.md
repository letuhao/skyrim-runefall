# The Runefall

*Told the way Skyrim tells everything: over fires, after funerals, and never
quite the same way twice. This is the telling the old riders agreed on, more
or less, because they were there for some of it - and everyone was there for
the rest.*

## The night with no thunder

It began on one of the coldest nights in living memory, and it began with
nothing. No thunder, no fires on the hills, no horn from any wall. The sky
over Skyrim cracked open for the length of a held breath, and through that
crack, runes fell - slow and silent, patient as the snow they came down
into. A watchman on the border counted one star too many and thought
nothing of it. A shepherd outside Falkreath swore the northern lights bent
to look at her. By dawn the sky had sealed itself, the snow lay unbroken,
and the whole province went back to work.

That was the trick of it. The runes did not declare themselves, and they
did not land - they sank. Into earth, into timber, into ice and
river-stone, into the deep bones of the old ruins, into anything with
roots, hunger or an edge. Nothing happened that day, or the day after, or
the month after. The land simply carried them from then on, the way a tide
carries salt: quietly, evenly, everywhere.

For more than a year, no one called it anything.

## The beasts first

Then the wild began to change - season by season, and always the wild
first.

A wolf in the Reach woke with a mark burning under its pelt and put to
flight the hunters who had tracked it all winter. A draugr rose from a
barrow it had guarded for four thousand years, its old bones now carved
with words no tongue of men ever taught it. A bear in the Rift took a
spear clean through the chest, walked it out, and made the hunter watch
her do it. Wolves held packs together that should have scattered. Mammoth
herds turned on patrols that had never once feared them.

Life did not change in a day. It got harder the way winter does - a
little, then a lot. Hunts came home smaller, then smaller still. Farmers
built their walls higher and posted children on the lookouts. Roads
learned to be walked in pairs, blades bare. There was no war to declare
and no border to rally behind; the enemy was simply the land's own teeth,
grown sharper in the dark. Skyrim began counting its dead quietly, and
refusing to pretend the count had always been this kind.

People named it the way names happen - after the night that started it:
**the Runefall**. The fall of the runes.

## The fragments

Everything turned when the hunters turned the tables. A hunt out of
Eastmarch put down a marked ice-wolf that had eaten through half their
sledges - and found, among the hide and bone, a fragment: a small piece of
whatever the sky had let fall, humming with the same old slow power, warm
in the palm in the worst cold they knew.

The wolf's mark had been a boast. The fragment was a trade, and the wild
still does not know it made one. People carried fragments out of the wild
the way they carried everything else out of it - stones, furs, grudges -
and used them the way folk use anything they do not yet understand: as
trophies, then as charms. A fen-wife sewed one into a hem and frost-rot
left the wound alone. A dockhand in Windhelm wore one on a cord through a
winter the old men called mild, and did not shiver once. Every hold came
around to the same quiet question: the beasts drink this power whole. Why
should everybody else die thirsty?

## The mages

The Winterhold mages had been working on that question since the first
week. They could not catch a rune in the sky - the sky was clean and the
runes were gone - but where the fallen power gathered strongest, in the
fragments, it could still be found, measured, weighed. Not magic as the
College teaches it. Not the Thu'um. Not the old tongue, not prophecy, not
a sickness. Simply old, unclaimed, and newly awake.

Years of careful, undramatic work followed - and their discovery changed
Skyrim more quietly than the sky-crack had. The power in a fragment does
not care whose body carries it. It had never belonged to the beasts
alone; the beasts had simply found it first, and never had to ask. A
fragment's strength could pass into a person - not by swallowing and not
by scarring, but by *welcoming*: offered a settled place in the body,
honored the way a guest is honored, drawn in slowly, over months, the way
a fever breaks instead of burns.

Not everyone could do it, and not every body could hold it. But the ones
who could - mages first, as it happens, and the first anywhere to try -
learned to take the runes' own power in and keep it, will against will.
They gave the practice a name, and the name traveled tavern by tavern,
hold by hold, because for the first time since the sky cracked, ordinary
people had been handed a way to fight back: **Rune Mastery**.

## The smiths

In Whiterun hold, a smith who had buried two customers to marked beasts
made a different wager. If a fragment could be welcomed into a living
body, she reasoned, it could be offered a different kind of host: a
blade, bound whole. Steel had carried blessings, spirits and names for a
thousand years - no one had ever asked it to hold a piece of the fallen
sky.

Her first attempt was an axe, and it went hunting with her. The bound
fragment woke when steel met marked hide and held its light through the
whole fight; the wolf went down the way any wolf does, instead of the way
the marked ones had been going down all winter. Other smiths argued with
the result, then argued with her method, then quietly built forges of
their own and stopped arguing. Weapons first; the craft spread to armor
within the year - plate, hide, the rings beneath - all of it bound, all
of it listening. The forge that first bound a rune whole into metal still
stands, and it gave the craft its name: the **Rune Forge**.

## Every craft opens its own door

Because the runes sank into everything, every craft that touches anything
was going to touch them back.

The enchanters had it strange and were paid best: fragments did not
overwrite the words already written into a ring or a robe - they
*deepened* them, the way deep snow keeps a warmth the frost never gets
at. Skyrim's oldest art came out of the Runefall holding things it had
never held, and the guilds learned to guide that deepening as a
discipline of its own.

The alchemists, whose work is mostly the land itself, found that the land
had changed under their feet: rune-trace now rode the waters of certain
springs, the blood of marked beasts, the sap of trees that grew near the
old ruins. They learned to find it, keep it, and brew it - and Skyrim's
medicine discovered it had a second opinion to offer.

That is why the runes reach so far, into so many hands: they never
belonged to one trade. They fell on the whole land at once, and the whole
land has been finding its own way to their use ever since.

## The land now

That was some years ago.

Today, the province a traveler walks into has been living with the
Runefall for years. Farmers watched the lookouts until the children grew
taller than the walls they watched from. The College teaches what it
learned the hard way. Forges from Rorikstead to Riften burn on questions
their founders never had to ask. Caravans trade fragments and the things
fragments have made, and pay honest coin for the deepest, meanest hunts.
The wild is still winning some nights. People mostly call that a fact
now, not a scandal.

And lately, the roads have been carrying a rumor: a stranger has crossed
the border.

## The lintel

Over the door of the first Rune Forge, before its hearth had ever lit,
one line was cut deep into hard stone. Every forge raised since has cut
it again, and every tavern in the province can finish it before you can:

*Master the runes - or be mastered by them.*
